Porto Seguro — where classical ML stops and neural nets start

The most-cited tabular competition in which neural networks beat gradient boosting. 595,212 rows · 57 anonymised features · normalised Gini · ~3.6% positive class.

0 · The data, in thirty seconds

PropertyValueWhy it decides everything downstream
Rows595,212 trainLarge for tabular. This is the first precondition for a neural net being competitive at all. At 10k rows it would not be.
Features57, all anonymisedNamed ps_ind_*, ps_reg_*, ps_car_*, ps_calc_*. No domain meaning is available, so hand-crafted feature engineering — the usual GBDT superpower — is mostly unavailable.
Suffixes_bin, _cat, else continuous/ordinalThe only schema hint you get. _cat columns include high-cardinality ones — the exact case where embeddings beat one-hot.
Missingencoded as -1Not NaN. Load it naively and -1 becomes a real numeric value that poisons every scaler and every distance.
Targetbinary, ~3.6% positiveHeavily imbalanced. Accuracy is meaningless — predict all-zero and score 96%.
Metricnormalised GiniA ranking metric (= 2·AUC − 1). You are scored on ordering risk, not on calibrated probabilities.
ps_calc_* group~20 columnsWidely reported as pure noise; top solutions dropped them. (reported by multiple write-ups; not re-measured here)
Why this competition is the right one for your question. Three conditions had to hold simultaneously for a neural net to win a tabular contest: a lot of rows, no usable domain knowledge to hand-engineer, and high-cardinality categoricals. Porto Seguro is the rare dataset with all three. On Titanic none of them hold, which is exactly why gradient boosting wins there.

1 · Track A — the all-ML approach, and where it stopped

Track A is not wrong. It is the correct first move, it produces the number everything else is judged against, and the eventual winner still kept one of its models in his final blend. What follows is where each stage runs out of room.

A1 · Profile and clean the datathe part nobody skips and everybody rushes

Nothing here is neural-net specific. Every model on both tracks needs this, and the cardinality print in the last line is what later decides one-hot versus embedding.
A2 · Linear baselinethe floor
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

Where it stops: one hyperplane over 57 columns. No interactions, no thresholds, and every _cat column has to be one-hot expanded first or it is read as a fake ordering — ps_car_11_cat = 104 is not "greater than" = 12.
A3 · Gradient boosting — the real Track A answerpublic kernels reached LB ≈ 0.279–0.284
nb ▸ andreylisin · LightGBM + XGBoost stacked (LB .279) nb ▸ tilii7 · Bayesian optimisation of XGBoost 
import lightgbm as lgb
X = train.drop(columns=[c for c in train if c.startswith('ps_calc')])  # widely reported as noise
# trees need NO scaling, NO imputation, NO one-hot for low-cardinality
model = lgb.LGBMClassifier(n_estimators=2000, learning_rate=0.02,
                           num_leaves=31, subsample=.8, colsample_bytree=.8)
Three lines of preprocessing, because trees are indifferent to scale, monotone transforms and raw missing markers. That convenience is precisely what makes Track A the correct starting point.
Where it stops — four structural ceilings:
1. High-cardinality categoricals. A split on a 100-level anonymised code is a split on one arbitrary subset. Trees have no way to place similar categories near each other, because they have no notion of "near".
2. No learned representation. A fitted GBDT is a pile of thresholds. Nothing inside it can be extracted and reused by another model. Compare Track B, where the embedding matrix is a reusable artefact.
3. Features are anonymised. The usual escape hatch — engineer smarter columns — is closed. You cannot build debt_to_income out of ps_ind_03.
4. Tuning is exhausted quickly. The Bayesian-optimisation kernel exists to demonstrate this: once the search is done, the ceiling is the model class, not the hyperparameters.
A4 · Ensemble everything18th place: CV Gini 0.2924 · test 0.2916
write-up ▸ jeddy92 · 18th place, "Safety in Numbers"
Verified from that write-up: roughly 40 models vetted, 16 kept in the final ensemble — XGBoost, LightGBM, Regularised Greedy Forests, Field-Aware Factorisation Machines and an entity-embedding neural net, combined by an L2-regularised logistic-regression meta-learner.
Where it stops: this is the top of Track A, and it took forty models to get there. Notice what is already inside that ensemble — an entity-embedding neural net. Even the best purely-classical route needed a neural component to reach the top twenty. The author's own conclusion, quoted from the write-up: with the right preparation and structural design, neural networks "could match or exceed traditional boosting performance on tabular data."

Read this before you get excited. The whole spread from a decent public GBDT kernel to the winning solution is roughly 0.017 Gini, and from a strong classical ensemble to the winner is about 0.004. Neural networks did not crush gradient boosting here — they edged past it, at the cost of a denoising autoencoder, a custom normalisation and five networks.

And the winning blend still contained a LightGBM. Track B did not replace Track A. It added a differently-wrong model whose errors were uncorrelated enough to be worth averaging in.

3 · Track B — how the neural nets got past Track A

Three specific mechanisms, each of which a tree structurally cannot perform. This is the answer to "how did Track B get further" — not "it was deeper", but these three things.

B1 · Entity embeddings — categories become geometrytrees cannot do this
nb ▸ aquatic · Entity Embedding Neural Net nb ▸ veerendrab · Using Entity Embeddings paper ▸ Guo & Berkhahn, Entity Embeddings 
# one-hot: 104 categories -> 104 orthogonal columns, all equidistant.
#          the model can never learn that category 12 behaves like category 47.
# embedding: 104 categories -> a learned 8-dimensional vector each.
#          similar-behaving categories drift together during training.

import torch.nn as nn
emb_dim = lambda n: min(50, (n + 1) // 2)      # common heuristic, cardinality -> width
emb = nn.ModuleList([nn.Embedding(n, emb_dim(n)) for n in cardinalities])
Why this is the core mechanism. The embedding is learned from the target, so it is a supervised dimensionality reduction. And it is an extractable artefact: per the jeddy92 write-up, the embeddings were pulled out of the network and fed back into LightGBM as features. That is Track B directly upgrading Track A — the exact thing you asked about.
B2 · RankGauss — the normalisation that made the nets trainablewinner's step
source ▸ winner's solution summary repo ▸ bluesky314 · representation learning 
from scipy.special import erfinv
def rank_gauss(x):
    r = x.argsort().argsort()                  # ranks
    r = (r / (len(r) - 1) - 0.5) * 2 * 0.998   # -> (-1, 1), endpoints pulled in
    return erfinv(r)                           # -> approximately Gaussian

# quoted from the winner's summary: rank transform, then "inverse of error function
# ErfInv to shape them like Gaussian". Better than standard scaling for gradient-based
# models; tree models are unaffected by it.
The bluesky314 README reports GaussRank reaching "half the loss in 2 epochs versus 500 with standard normalisation". Treat that as one repo's claim, not a benchmark — but the direction is consistent with the winner independently choosing the same transform.
This is the cleanest illustration of the whole page. The same transform that is decisive for a neural net is invisible to a tree, because trees split on order and RankGauss preserves order exactly. Preprocessing is not a fixed checklist — it is a function of which model you are feeding.
B3 · Denoising autoencoder with swap noise — the winning move1st place, 0.2965
forum ▸ Jahrer · "1st place with representation learning" 
# swap noise: replace 15% of values with values drawn from ANOTHER ROW
# of the same column. Gaussian noise is wrong for tabular data - it would
# invent category 3.7. Swapping keeps every value legal.
mask = np.random.rand(*X.shape) < 0.15
X_noisy = np.where(mask, X[np.random.permutation(len(X))], X)

# 1. train a DAE on train+test together to reconstruct X from X_noisy
# 2. throw away the reconstruction; keep the HIDDEN ACTIVATIONS as new features
#    ("deep stack": concatenate activations of all hidden layers -> 1k-10k features)
# 3. train the supervised MLP on those activations, never on the raw columns
Verified details of the supervised nets from the solution summary: hidden layer size 1000, ReLU, sigmoid output, plain SGD with learning-rate decay, MSE loss, input dropout. Final submission: 1 LightGBM + 5 neural nets, equal weight, private 0.2965. He reported that XGBoost, nonlinear stacking, bagging, GANs and most manual feature engineering did not work for him.
Why an autoencoder at all? The features are anonymised, so no human can engineer good ones. So he had the network engineer them — unsupervised, using train and test together, learning the joint structure of the columns before ever looking at the target. That is what "representation learning" means in his title, and it is the thing gradient boosting has no equivalent of.

4 · Cleaning and preprocessing — ML versus NN, step by step

The single most useful table on this page. Same dataset, same eight steps, two very different answers, because the two model families fail in different ways.

#StepFor gradient boostingFor a neural netWhat breaks if you use the tree answer on a net

The rule underneath the table. Trees are invariant to any monotone transform of a single feature, and indifferent to scale and to missing markers. Neural nets are invariant to none of those. So "cleaning" is not one pipeline you write once — it is a function of the model you are about to feed. The winner's RankGauss step is worth nothing to a tree and decisive for a net; that is the whole idea in one example.
The NN preprocessing pipeline, in order
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from scipy.special import erfinv
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold

df = pd.read_csv('train.csv')
y  = df.pop('target'); df.pop('id')

# 1. drop the noise group
df = df.drop(columns=[c for c in df if c.startswith('ps_calc')])

# 2. -1 means missing. record THAT it was missing, then fill.
df = df.replace(-1, np.nan)
for c in df.columns[df.isna().any()]:
    df[c + '_isna'] = df[c].isna().astype(np.float32)
cat = [c for c in df if c.endswith('_cat')]
num = [c for c in df if c not in cat and not c.endswith('_bin')
                     and not c.endswith('_isna')]
df[cat] = df[cat].fillna(-1).astype('int32')       # own level for "missing"
df[num] = df[num].fillna(df[num].median())

# 3. RankGauss the continuous columns  (fit on TRAIN ONLY, per fold)
def rank_gauss(x):
    r = x.argsort().argsort()
    return erfinv((r / (len(r) - 1) - .5) * 2 * .998)
for c in num:
    df[c] = rank_gauss(df[c].values)

# 4. categoricals -> contiguous codes, ready for nn.Embedding
for c in cat:
    df[c] = pd.factorize(df[c])[0]
cards = [int(df[c].max()) + 1 for c in cat]

# 5. stratified folds - the positive class is only 3.6%
cv = StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
Steps 2 and 3 are the two that a tree pipeline would simply not contain. If you are converting a working GBDT script into a neural-net script, those are the lines you are missing.

5 · The neural-net ladder for tabular data

Smallest model first. Climb only when you can state, in your own measured numbers, the wall the current rung hit. A rung climbed without a wall teaches nothing, because you never learn what the previous model could not do.

Rung 0 · Constant predictor Gini 0.0
Predict the base rate for everyone. Gini is 0 by definition. Anything at 0 is broken, not undertrained. Two lines, and it calibrates your instinct for what the metric means.
You have a floor but no model.
Rung 1 · Logistic regression baseline
Standardise, one-hot the low-cardinality categoricals. This is a single-layer perceptron with a sigmoid — the literal ancestor. Everything above must beat it or has a bug.
One hyperplane. No interactions, no thresholds. The XOR wall, on 57 columns.
Rung 2 · LightGBM your real bar ≈ 0.279+
Not a neural net, and it belongs here anyway. This is the number a tabular neural net has to justify itself against. Skip it and you will never know whether your net is good or merely working.
Ceiling on high-cardinality categoricals; nothing learned is reusable; features are anonymised so engineering is blocked.
Rung 3 · Plain MLP — input → 256 → 128 → 1
Fully-connected, ReLU, dropout 0.3, BatchNorm, Adam 1e-3, one-hot categoricals. Expect it to lose to rung 2, and expect that to be the norm on tabular data. Its job is to prove your pipeline runs end to end.
nb ▸ rspadim · Gini + Keras EarlyStopping nb ▸ tomcwalker · Keras NN, custom Gini/AUC loss
One-hot on high-cardinality columns wastes width and learns no similarity between levels. This is the wall that matters.
Rung 4 · MLP + entity embeddings first rung that earns its place
One embedding per _cat column, concatenated with the RankGauss'd numerics, then the same dense head. Per the bluesky314 README, send the numeric block through its own fully-connected layer first so the embeddings do not dominate the concatenation. This is the first architecture doing something LightGBM structurally cannot.
nb ▸ aquatic · Entity Embedding Neural Net nb ▸ veerendrab · Entity Embeddings and NNs
Competitive with rung 2, rarely a clear win alone. And the numeric columns are still raw inputs — nothing has learned their structure.
Rung 5 · Blend rung 4 with rung 2 the cheapest real gain
Average the two rank-normalised prediction vectors. They fail on different rows, so the blend beats both. Do this before anything more complicated. The winner's final submission was exactly this idea: 1 LightGBM + 5 neural nets, equal weights, 0.2965.
You have squeezed the models you have. Further gain needs better features, and the columns are anonymised.
Rung 6 · Feed embeddings back into LightGBM
Extract the trained embedding vectors, append them as ordinary numeric columns, retrain the GBDT. Reported in the jeddy92 write-up as part of an 18th-place ensemble. Track B improving Track A, concretely.
Embeddings only describe the categorical columns. The numeric block is still unrepresented.
Rung 7 · Denoising autoencoder + supervised MLP 1st place · 0.2965
Swap noise at 15%, DAE trained on train+test features, hidden activations taken as the new feature matrix (1k–10k wide), then MLPs with hidden width 1000 trained on those activations. Expensive, and the top of the ladder for a reason.
forum ▸ Jahrer · 1st place with representation learning summary ▸ kaggler.com
Where to actually stop. Rung 5 is the honest destination for a first pass. Rungs 6 and 7 are the competition endgame — worth reading in full, worth implementing only once rungs 1 through 5 are all measured and on paper. If your rung 3 MLP loses to rung 2 LightGBM, that is not a failure; that is the expected result, and it is the whole reason rung 4 exists.
